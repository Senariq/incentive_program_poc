03 · What We Believe Is Possible

Put investment where it can change the outcome.

Revenue is the north star. Sales volume, retention, and engagement help explain how a program may move it—and which participants or rewards merit investment.

Hypothesis · not a promise
DESIGN HYPOTHESIS 01

Learn what feels valuable

Points and intrinsic-value redemptions, public recognition, sweepstakes, and winner experiences may motivate differently than cash. Treat each as a testable program hypothesis.

DESIGN HYPOTHESIS 02

Move the middle; sustain the top

Test which support helps middle-tier participants advance and what helps top performers sustain results. Do not assume top performers need rewards—or that lower performers will respond to the same lever.

DESIGN HYPOTHESIS 03

Allocate for incremental return

Compare the incremental outcome and cost of alternative investments across segments before shifting budget or scaling a reward.

What must be true?Set incremental revenue as the primary outcome where appropriate; agree which retention, sales-volume, and engagement measures support it or act as guardrails. Do not combine KPIs into one score without client-approved weights, reward costs, and participant protections.

04 · How Decision Sciences Can Help

Methods are useful when they answer your question.

Each capability has a job in the decision process. The measure is not how many analyses we run; it is whether the evidence helps you choose what to do.

Tools in service of decisions
Understand change

Pre / post analysis

Establish what moved around a program or intervention—and what else may explain the change.

Find variation

Cohort analysis

See where response differs so a single average does not hide distinct participant needs.

Evaluate a claim

Hypothesis testing

Make assumptions explicit and assess whether the observed evidence supports them.

Test fairly

Practical experiments

When approved, compare a reward or communication variation with clear, consistent eligibility. If individual randomization is not acceptable, consider a phased rollout or matched comparison and state its limits.

Guide action

Decision dashboards

Surface the measures, thresholds, and exceptions that help owners take the next step.

Make it relevant

Program-specific measures

Fit the measures to the client: employee recognition and retention, sales growth, customer engagement, learning, or self-selected goals.

→
Fairness is part of the design

Start with client-approved, transparent rules and guardrails. A pilot can compare program options or rollout timing without changing core eligibility; use historical or matched comparisons when a controlled test is not approved.

07 · Where We Go From Here

Start with one outcome. Prove the next step.

A focused pilot can turn the conversation into a measurable decision—without asking you to commit to an unproven return.

A practical path forward
01
Align on the business question

Choose the outcome, participant audience, and decision the pilot should inform.

02
Confirm data, measures, and fairness guardrails

Agree on approved data, incremental outcome measures, participant protections, and a comparison approach clients can support.

03
Choose a low-friction first test

Compare reward or recognition options, or use a phased rollout; preserve transparent eligibility and account for reward and delivery cost.

04
Let the program owner act on the evidence

Review incremental results with the relevant HR, Growth, Marketing, or Finance sponsor; refine, scale, or stop the investment.
